The Karachi

Ask ten people what Karachi is, and you'll get ten different answers. To some, it's Pakistan's beating economic heart the port that keeps the country's trade moving, the banks and stock exchange that decide how much money the rest of the nation has to spend. To others, it's simply home: a sprawling, restless, endlessly noisy city where more than 20 million people wake up every morning and somehow make it work.

Karachi sits at the southern edge of Pakistan, where the land finally gives way to the Arabian Sea. It's the capital of Sindh province, but its influence has never stayed within provincial lines for twelve years after independence in 1947, it was the capital of the entire country, before that title moved north to Islamabad. That history still shows up in the city's bones: old government buildings standing next to newer high-rises, colonial-era architecture sharing streets with construction that never seems to stop.

Numbers alone don't capture what Karachi is, but they hint at its weight. It's the largest city in Pakistan by a wide margin and one of the largest in the world, period the 11th, depending on how you count. Its economy is estimated at over $200 billion, and it's classified as a beta-global city, a label reserved for places that matter far beyond their own borders. None of that happened by accident. Karachi is where much of Pakistan's industry is based, where a huge share of the country's tax revenue is collected, and where people from every province come looking for work.

That last part is what really sets the city apart. Walk through Karachi and you'll hear Urdu, Sindhi, Pashto, Punjabi, and half a dozen other languages within a few blocks of each other. You'll pass mosques, churches, temples, and shrines, often close enough to share a wall. It's the most ethnically and religiously mixed city in the country, and arguably the most socially open a place where lifestyles and opinions coexist that wouldn't always sit comfortably elsewhere in Pakistan. That mix is exactly what makes it complicated, and exactly what makes it worth understanding.

Karachi Is Getting Hotter And It's Not an Accident

There's a difference between a hot summer and a dangerous one, and Karachi crossed that line a while ago. Heat used to be a season here. Now it's a threat.

In June 2015, the city recorded a maximum temperature of 44.8°C but with humidity and dead air trapping it in place, the actual heat index climbed to around 66°C on the worst day. Over 1,200 people died in less than a week. It wasn't a freak event. Karachi was hit again in 2018, and again in March 2022, when temperatures broke records that had stood since 1901.

Here's the part most people get wrong: Karachi isn't just hot because the planet is warming. It's hot because of what the city has done to itself.

Scientists have a name for it the urban heat island effect. Concrete, asphalt, and glass soak up solar heat all day and release it slowly through the night, so the city never really cools down. Satellite data shows Karachi's core running up to 5°C hotter than its outskirts, and the gap keeps widening as the city keeps building outward. In neighborhoods where dense, unplanned construction has blocked the sea breeze that once cooled the coast, the heat simply has nowhere to go.

It doesn't stop at concrete. Along the coast, the mangrove forests that once buffered Karachi from storms and heat have lost more than 40% of their cover since the 1970s of eight native species recorded decades ago, four have vanished completely. Inland, tree cover has been steadily traded for construction, stripping away the shade and natural cooling the city used to have for free. And even where trees have been planted, it hasn't always helped Karachi has a history of choosing fast, cheap species over the right ones, a decision with consequences that are still playing out today.

None of this is unfixable. But it starts with understanding what's actually happening beneath the heat not just that Karachi is hot, but why, and what's quietly disappearing because of it.
